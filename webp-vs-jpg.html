WebP vs JPG: which is smaller?

For the same photo at the same visual quality, WebP is usually 25–35% smaller than JPG. Here are real numbers, the cases where JPG still wins, and where AVIF fits in.

The numbers: same photo, matched quality

Quality scales are not directly comparable between formats, so these were matched by visual inspection — zoomed in, no visible difference. Typical results for a 1600×1200 photograph:

JPG at quality 80: ~280KB. The baseline everyone knows.
WebP at quality 80: ~190KB. Roughly one-third smaller than the JPG.
AVIF at quality 60: ~150KB. Nearly half the JPG's size.

These are typical values, not guarantees — a noisy low-light photo compresses worse in every format, and a simple graphic compresses better. The ranking stays the same though: AVIF smallest, WebP in the middle, JPG largest, at equal visual quality.

Why WebP files are smaller

WebP uses a newer compression design (derived from the VP8 video codec) that predicts image content more efficiently than JPG's 1990s-era DCT approach. It also does two things JPG cannot: transparency like PNG, and animation like GIF — both at much smaller sizes than those older formats. For a website, that means one format can replace three.

When JPG still wins

Smaller is not always better. JPG wins on compatibility: every device, browser, printer driver and government upload portal made in the last 30 years accepts JPG. Some exam and visa portals explicitly reject WebP uploads — for those, convert to JPG first and check the exam photo size guide for the exact limits. JPG also renders progressively on slow connections, showing a blurry preview that sharpens, which some readers prefer.

Rule of thumb: if a human or an old system will open the file, use JPG. If a modern browser will display it on a website, use WebP.

When WebP wins

WebP is the right default for anything displayed on the web: blog images, product photos, thumbnails, hero banners. A page whose images are one-third smaller loads visibly faster on mobile data, and page speed feeds directly into both user experience and search rankings. WebP also handles transparency at a fraction of PNG's file size — a logo with a transparent background that needs 400KB as PNG is often under 100KB as WebP with no visible difference.

Browser support in 2026

WebP is supported by every modern browser — Chrome, Edge and Firefox since 2018–2019, Safari since 2020. Unless your audience uses very old software, support is a solved problem. AVIF support is also broad now (all major browsers since 2021–2022), but encoding is slower and a few tools still choke on it, so WebP remains the safer modern choice for general use.

Where AVIF fits

AVIF compresses better than WebP — often 20–30% smaller again at the same quality — but it encodes much more slowly and is less universally supported in desktop software. Use AVIF for large hero images where every kilobyte counts and you control the delivery; use WebP everywhere else. Frequently asked questions

Is WebP always smaller than JPG?

At matched visual quality, yes — typically 25–35% smaller for photographs. For tiny thumbnails under a few kilobytes the difference shrinks, and for simple graphics PNG or WebP-lossless may beat both.

Will WebP work everywhere?

In browsers, yes — all modern ones support it. In desktop software, old portals and some government forms, no: a few still reject WebP. When in doubt, submit JPG.

Should I convert all my JPGs to WebP?

For images served on a website, yes — it is one of the cheapest speed wins available. For archival photos, keep the originals; every re-encode between lossy formats loses a little detail.

What about AVIF vs WebP?

AVIF files are smaller but slower to encode and slightly less supported. WebP is the practical default in 2026; AVIF is the specialist choice for maximum compression.
